P75 [Normal] ABSTRACT 
P77 [Body Text] Smart hospitals increasingly depend on connected medical devices, sensors and digital clinical systems. These components generate heterogeneous data that can be useful for identifying unusual device behaviour and potential operational risks. However, healthcare data is sensitive, distributed across institutions and difficult to combine centrally. Conventional centralized machine-learning pipelines may require raw data to be transferred to a common server, creating privacy, governance and communication concerns. In addition, IoT telemetry and clinical text have different structures and require suitable representation methods.
P79 [Body Text] This project presents a multi-modal anomaly-detection framework that combines semantic vectorization and federated graph learning for smart-hospital IoT environments. The design uses Bio_ClinicalBERT for clinical text representation and GraphSAGE for learning relationships among connected IoT devices. Federated learning is used to coordinate learning across three simulated hospital clients through the Federated Averaging (FedAvg) approach. The intended design keeps raw client data local while allowing model information to be aggregated for collaborative learning.
P81 [Body Text] The reported experiments examine predictive performance, modality contribution, communication cost and robustness under a severe non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) setting. The recorded results include a federated IoT F1-score of 99.30%, an IoT-only F1-score of 99.26% and a PR-AUC of 0.9942. In the modality comparison, IoT-only, clinical-only and combined settings achieved 97.74%, 50.33% and 97.64%, respectively, for the reported comparison metric. Over ten rounds and three clients, communication was reported as 0.97 MB for IoT-only federated learning and 31.03 MB for the multimodal federated configuration. The worst-client F1-score under severe non-IID data was 0.9630. These results indicate strong IoT-side predictive performance and highlight the communication overhead introduced by multimodal model exchange. The report also discusses limitations, reproducibility requirements and future improvements.

P147 [Normal] 2. LITERATURE SURVEY
P149 [Normal] 2.1 BACKGROUND AND RELATED CONCEPTS 
P151 [Normal] 2.1.1 Internet of Medical Things 
P152 [Normal] The Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) refers to connected devices and systems used to collect, transmit or process healthcare-related information. Examples include bedside monitors, wearable sensors, smart infusion equipment and environmental monitoring systems. These devices can improve visibility into hospital operations, but their connectivity also creates a large and varied data environment. 
P153 [Normal] Anomaly detection in IoMT may identify unusual measurements, unexpected device states, abnormal communication patterns or deviations from normal operating behaviour. The meaning of an anomaly depends on context. A measurement that is unusual for one device or patient setting may be normal in another. For this reason, the relationships between devices and the operating environment can be useful signals.
P155 [Normal] 2.1.2 Anomaly Detection 
P156 [Normal] Anomaly detection is the process of identifying observations or patterns that differ significantly from expected behaviour. In supervised settings, labelled examples can be used to train a classifier. In unsupervised or semi-supervised settings, the model may learn a representation of normal behaviour and flag deviations. The project’s reported F1-score and PR-AUC indicate that classification-style evaluation was used for at least part of the experimental analysis; the precise label-generation and split protocol should be documented from the experiment logs.
P157 [Normal] Accuracy alone can be misleading when abnormal events are rare. Precision measures the proportion of predicted anomalies that are correct, while recall measures the proportion of actual anomalies detected. The F1-score combines precision and recall through their harmonic mean. Precision–Recall AUC summarizes the precision-recall trade-off across thresholds and is particularly informative for imbalanced binary classification.
P159 [Normal] 2.1.3 Graph Neural Networks and GraphSAGE 
P160 [Normal] Many real-world systems can be represented as graphs. A graph consists of nodes and edges, where nodes represent entities and edges represent relationships. In a hospital IoT environment, nodes may represent connected devices and edges may represent relationships defined by the dataset or system topology. Graph neural networks learn node representations by combining information from a node and its neighbourhood. GraphSAGE is an inductive graph representation method that samples and aggregates features from neighbouring nodes. Its inductive nature can support representations for nodes not seen during the initial training, provided that suitable features and neighbourhood information are available. In this project, GraphSAGE is the graph-learning component for IoT relationships. The exact node attributes, edge definitions, sampling sizes, aggregation function and number of layers must be taken from the implementation configuration. These implementation-specific details are intentionally not guessed in this report
P162 [Normal] 2.1.4 Clinical Language Models 
P163 [Normal] Clinical text contains abbreviations, domain-specific terms, contextual references and phrases whose meaning depends on surrounding words. Traditional bag-of-words methods may fail to preserve these contextual relationships. Transformer-based language models use attention mechanisms to build contextual token representations. Bio_ClinicalBERT is a BERT-family language model further trained on clinical text. It can be used to produce contextual representations of clinical notes or text segments. In the proposed system, semantic vectorization provides a numerical representation of clinical language that can be used by downstream learning components. The report does not assume a particular pooling strategy or embedding dimension unless confirmed from the code. 
P165 [Normal] 2.1.5 Federated Learning 
P166 [Normal] Federated learning is a distributed machine-learning approach in which multiple clients train locally and share model updates with a coordinating server. The server aggregates the updates and distributes a global model for subsequent training. This approach can reduce the need to transfer raw client data to a central repository. 
P167 [Normal] FedAvg, introduced by McMahan and colleagues, performs weighted averaging of locally trained model parameters. In a typical round, the server sends the current global parameters to selected clients. Each client trains using its local data and returns updated parameters. The server aggregates the updates, commonly weighting each client according to its number of training examples.
P171 [Normal] 2.2 EXISTING SYSTEM AND ITS LIMITATIONS
P172 [Normal] A common approach to multi-site machine learning is to collect data from participating institutions into a centralized storage and processing environment. A central model can then be trained using the pooled dataset. This approach can simplify model development and provide direct access to combined data,but it requires data transfer and centralized governance.

P308 [Normal] 4.3 EVALUATION METRICS
P310 [Normal] 4.3.1 Precision
P311 [Normal] Precision measures how many of the instances predicted as positive are actually positive. It is calculated as TP / (TP + FP), where TP is the number of true positives and FP is the number of false positives. High precision indicates that fewer normal observations are incorrectly flagged as anomalies.
P315 [Normal] 4.3.2 Recall
P316 [Normal] Recall measures how many actual positive instances are correctly detected. It is calculated as TP / (TP + FN), where FN is the number of false negatives. In anomaly detection, recall is important because missed abnormal events may be operationally significant.
P318 [Normal] 4.3.3 F1-score
P319 [Normal] The F1-score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. It is calculated as 2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall). It provides a single measure that balances false positives and false negatives. The project reports an IoT federated F1-score of 99.30% and an IoT-only F1-score of 99.26% in the overall result summary.
P321 [Normal] 4.3.4 Precision–Recall AUC
P322 [Normal] The area under the precision-recall curve summarizes the relationship between precision and recall over different classification thresholds. It is useful when positive examples are relatively rare. The project reports a PR-AUC value of 0.9942 for the federated IoT result.
P324 [Normal] 4.3.5 Communication Cost
P325 [Normal] Communication cost measures the volume of information exchanged between clients and the aggregation server. It can be reported in bytes, kilobytes or megabytes. The measurement should define whether both upload and download traffic are included and whether protocol overhead is counted. The project reports 0.97 MB for IoT-only federated learning and 31.03 MB for multimodal federated learning over ten rounds and three clients.
P327 [Normal] 4.3.6 Worst-Client Performance
P328 [Normal] Worst-client performance is the lowest client-level metric among the participating clients. It is useful in federated settings because aggregate performance may hide poor results for a particular participant. The project reports a worst-client F1-score of 0.9630 under severe non-IID conditions.

Under severe non-IID conditions, the worst-client F1-score was 0.9630. These results provide evidence of strong performance in the evaluated IoT setting, while also showing that the combined modality did not outperform IoT-only in the reported comparison.
P392 [Normal] The communication experiment reported 0.97 MB for IoT-only federated learning and 31.03 MB for multimodal federated learning over ten rounds and three clients. This highlights the communication cost associated with the multimodal configuration. The project therefore demonstrates the importance of evaluating predictive performance, client fairness and communication efficiency together rather than focusing on a single headline metric.
P394 [Normal] 5.2 FUTURE SCOPE
P396 [Normal] 5.2.1 Privacy-Preserving Federated Learning
P397 [Normal] Future work can incorporate secure aggregation so that the server cannot directly inspect individual client updates. Differential privacy can also be studied by adding calibrated noise to updates or gradients and measuring the resulting privacy-utility trade-off. These mechanisms should be evaluated carefully because stronger privacy settings can reduce model performance.
P399 [Normal] 5.2.2 Communication Optimization
P400 [Normal] The communication overhead of the multimodal configuration can be reduced through update compression, quantization, sparsification, selective parameter sharing and adaptive communication frequency. Experiments should compare the reduction in transmitted bytes with any change in F1-score, PR-AUC and worst-client performance.
P402 [Normal] 5.2.3 Improved Multi-Modal Fusion
P403 [Normal] The modality comparison suggests that the fusion strategy deserves further investigation. Future experiments can evaluate late fusion, attention-based fusion, gated fusion and modality-specific normalization. The clinical branch should be checked for label alignment, missingness and domain shift before changing the architecture.
P405 [Normal] 5.2.4 Broader Non-IID Evaluation
P406 [Normal] The framework can be tested with more clients and a wider range of non-IID settings. Future analysis should report average, standard deviation and worst-client performance, along with per-client sample counts. This would provide a clearer picture of whether improvements are consistent across different hospital-like environments.
P408 [Normal] 5.2.5 Realistic Smart-Hospital Validation
P409 [Normal] A future extension can evaluate the framework using carefully governed data from multiple healthcare environments or realistic testbeds. Any such study must follow data-protection requirements, institutional approvals and appropriate anonymization or de-identification practices. Clinical and operational experts should review the definition of anomalies and the usefulness of model alerts.
